Principals and hiring committees usually want to know a few things right away: are you licensed for this subject and grade, what have you taught, and what changed for your students. This template answers those in that order. It puts licensure under the summary and uses the Compact layout, which fits twelve years of teaching at three schools on one page.

The sample belongs to a middle school science teacher. The same structure works for elementary, high school, special education and language teachers. Swap in your own details and delete the sections you don't need.

## What hiring managers look for

A principal reading your resume is trying to picture you in a real classroom in their building. They look for the grade levels and subjects you have taught, how you handle a room of students, how you work with colleagues and families, and whether your students learned.

Many districts screen applications through an online system before a principal sees them. Those systems check for the license area, the grade band and words from the posting, like "differentiated instruction" or "co-teaching." A resume that names these clearly gets through the first screen more often.

Leadership counts at every level. Running a club, mentoring a student teacher or leading a grade-level team shows that you add something beyond your own classroom.

## How to write each section

### Header and contact details

Your name, the role you want, city and state, email and phone. A link to an online portfolio with lesson plans or student work samples is a good addition if it is current. You don't need a full street address.

Use a title that matches the posting when it is true for you, for example "Middle School Science Teacher" or "Elementary Teacher, Grades K to 2."

### Summary

Two or three sentences: your grade levels and subjects, how many years you have taught and one thing that defines your teaching. These examples show the pattern at different levels:

> **First-year teacher:** Newly licensed elementary teacher with a full semester of student teaching in a grade 3 classroom. I plan hands-on math lessons, use small-group reading rotations and keep families updated every week.

> **Experienced teacher:** High school English teacher with seven years in grades 9 to 12. I teach writing as a process, run student-led discussions and have taught both AP Literature and support sections for struggling readers.

> **Department lead:** Science teacher with nine years in grades 6 to 8. I plan hands-on, lab-based units, use assessment data to regroup students every few weeks and lead a department of six teachers.

### Licensure

Write the license type, the subjects and grade bands it covers and the state that issued it. If your license is pending, write the expected date, for example "Ohio Resident Educator License, expected June 2026." Extra endorsements, like ESL, reading or special education, belong here too because many schools filter on them.

The sample places licensure right after the summary. A principal can confirm in seconds that you are eligible to teach the role.

### Experience

List schools in reverse order, newest first. Give the school name, your title, the city and the dates. Add one short line of context: grade levels, subjects, the number of sections or students, and anything special about the setting, such as inclusion classes or a bilingual program.

Then three to five bullets for your current role and one to three for older roles. Each bullet should show something you built, led or improved.

### Skills

Group skills into a few short labeled lines: teaching approaches, classroom practices and tools. The sample uses three groups. Keep each line to the things you could talk about with an example in an interview.

### Education

List your degrees with the university and the years. A master's degree in education, curriculum or a subject area goes first. For new teachers, a line about student teaching or a relevant thesis is fine here. Experienced teachers can keep this section to the degree names only.

## Writing experience bullets

Strong teacher bullets show what you did and what happened for students or colleagues. A simple pattern that works: **what you did + for whom + what changed.**

- Weak: "Taught science to middle school students."
- Better: "Rewrote the grade 8 physical science units around weekly labs; students meeting the state standard rose from 61% to 74% over two years."

If you don't have test data, describe other evidence: attendance in your club, reading levels, projects that went to a fair, or a routine other teachers adopted.

### Examples by level

**Student teacher or first-year teacher**

- Taught a full unit on fractions to a grade 4 class of 24 students under a mentor teacher
- Planned and led daily small-group reading for three groups at different levels
- Built a classroom behavior chart with the class and used it for the full semester
- Sent a weekly email update to families with what we learned and what was coming next

**Experienced teacher**

- Teach five sections of English 10, about 140 students a year, including one co-taught inclusion section
- Rewrote the persuasive writing unit around student debates; average essay scores rose by one rubric level
- Started the after-school robotics club, which now has 25 members
- Mentored two student teachers from the local university

**Department lead or coordinator**

- Lead the science department of six teachers: run monthly planning meetings and shared assessments
- Coordinated the move to a new curriculum across grades 6 to 8, including training for all teachers
- Built a common grading rubric for lab reports now used across the department
- Review new teachers' unit plans and observe each one twice a semester

### Examples by specialty

**Elementary**

- Teach all core subjects to a grade 2 class of 22 students
- Introduced reading conferences and home reading logs; most students moved up at least one reading level by spring
- Organized the grade-level family math night with three other teachers

**Middle school**

- Run advisory for a group of 18 grade 7 students and meet with each family twice a year
- Co-plan interdisciplinary units with the math and social studies teachers on my team
- Moved homework to short daily practice in class, which raised completion rates

**High school subject teacher**

- Teach AP Chemistry and two sections of general chemistry
- Raised the share of AP students scoring 3 or higher over three years by adding weekly practice exams
- Coach the Science Olympiad team, which placed at the state tournament

**Special education**

- Case manager for 14 students with IEPs: write goals, track progress and lead annual meetings
- Co-teach math and English in grades 6 to 8 with general education teachers
- Trained classroom aides on behavior support plans and data collection

**ESL or language teacher**

- Teach English language learners in grades 3 to 5 in pull-out and push-in settings
- Built a vocabulary program with picture cards in four home languages
- Translate key school messages and meet with families with an interpreter when needed

**Early childhood**

- Lead teacher for a pre-K classroom of 16 children aged 4 and 5
- Plan play-based units and track development with the school's observation checklist
- Hold family conferences twice a year and send a photo update every Friday

## Skills and keywords

District application systems often search for exact terms from the posting. Use the names people use in job posts, and only list what you have actually done in a classroom.

| Category | Common examples |
| --- | --- |
| Teaching approaches | Differentiated instruction, project-based learning, inquiry, co-teaching, small-group instruction |
| Assessment | Formative assessment, data-driven instruction, rubrics, progress monitoring, standards-based grading |
| Classroom management | Positive behavior support, restorative practices, routines, PBIS |
| Student support | IEPs, 504 plans, English language learners, intervention, RTI and MTSS |
| Family and community | Parent conferences, family communication, community events |
| Tools | Google Classroom, Canvas, Seesaw, PowerSchool, Nearpod, Desmos |

Patience and good communication matter in teaching, but a line that says "patient and caring" doesn't prove anything. Show those qualities in bullets: how you worked with a struggling student, how you kept families informed or how you supported a new colleague.

## Tailoring your resume to a job post

A short edit for each school works better than sending the same resume everywhere. It takes about ten minutes:

1. Read the posting and mark the grade levels, subjects, programs and terms it repeats.
2. Make sure your license area and grade band match, and that the match is easy to see.
3. Move the bullets closest to the role to the top of each job.
4. Adjust the summary so the first sentence names the role, for example "grade 3 teacher" for an elementary posting.
5. Mention programs the school uses if you know them, such as a reading curriculum, IB or a co-teaching model.

Use the posting's words when they are true for you. If the school asks for "co-teaching experience" and you have it, say "co-teaching", not only "worked with the special education team."

## Formatting and length

Keep it to one page unless you have a long career with leadership roles and several schools. Use one font, a size between 10 and 12 points and the same date format throughout. Avoid putting important information in tables, text boxes or images, since district application systems sometimes skip them.

Save and send the resume as a PDF unless the district asks for Word. Name the file clearly, for example "Hannah-Whitaker-Science-Teacher.pdf".

## Why the sections are in this order

The template puts licensure right after the summary because it is the first thing a principal checks. Experience comes next, then education, because after your first few years what you have done in a classroom says more than your degree.

New teachers can move education above experience and treat student teaching as the main job. The [student template](/templates/student) shows that approach for people early in their careers.

Skills sit below education. They support the bullets above them, so a principal who wants detail can find it, but they rarely decide an interview on their own.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Listing duties every teacher has, like "planned lessons and graded papers"
- Leaving out the grade level or subject
- An expired or missing license with no expected date
- Student names or details that could identify a child
- Educational jargon a principal outside your specialty may not know
- Results you can't explain in the interview
- Different date formats in different jobs
- A two-page resume early in your career when one page covers it

## Checklist before you send it

- Your title and summary match the grade level and subject in the posting
- Your license, subjects and grade bands are easy to find
- Every recent job has at least one bullet with a result or a clear outcome
- The terms from the posting that apply to you appear on the page
- No student names or identifying details anywhere
- Dates, spelling and school names are consistent
- The file is a PDF with your name in the file name
